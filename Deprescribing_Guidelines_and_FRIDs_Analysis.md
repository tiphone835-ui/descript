## 1. Executive Summary & Purpose

### 1.1 Purpose and Population Scope
* **Objective:** Translate international clinical evidence into actionable, safe, and feasible deprescribing recommendations for older adults.
* **Target Population:**
  * Adults aged **$\ge 65$ years** taking at least one regular chronic medication.
  * Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples aged **$\ge 50$ years**.
* **Medication Scope:** Focuses primarily on the **top 100 PBS-dispensed medicines** (by volume and unique patient count) in Australia, alongside high-risk or commonly overprescribed classes (e.g., psychotropics, digoxin, anticholinergics).

### 1.2 Core Philosophy
* **Deprescribing Defined:** A person-centred process of supervised medication withdrawal intended to achieve improved health outcomes through the discontinuation or dose reduction of medications that are potentially harmful, ineffective, or no longer indicated.
* **Shared Decision-Making:** Deprescribing is preference-sensitive and requires informed consent, exploring patient values, and collaborative planning among the patient, carers, general practitioners, pharmacists, and medical specialists.

## 2. Guiding Clinical Frameworks

### 2.1 The 5Ms Geriatric Framework
All deprescribing discussions are structured around the **5Ms**:
1. **Mind:** Cognition, dementia, delirium, mood disorders, and psychiatric burden.
2. **Mobility:** Gait, balance, prevention of falls and associated fractures.
3. **Medicines:** Inappropriate polypharmacy, adverse drug interactions, treatment burden, and prescribing cascades.
4. **Multicomplexity:** Multimorbidity and overlapping biopsychosocial conditions.
5. **What Matters Most:** Aligning pharmacotherapy directly with the patient’s individual health outcome goals and life expectancy.

### 2.2 Prioritisation Matrix for Deprescribing
When an older adult takes multiple medications, the guideline prioritises withdrawal using a $2 \times 2$ balance:
* **Stop First:** High risk of harm from continued use + Low ongoing benefit + Low risk of adverse drug withdrawal events (ADWEs).
* **Stop Last / Exercise Extreme Caution:** Low risk of harm from continued use + High ongoing benefit + High risk of severe ADWEs upon cessation.

### 2.3 Recognising Prescribing Cascades
A key principle is intercepting prescribing cascades, where drug side effects are misdiagnosed as new clinical conditions:
* *Example 1:* Calcium Channel Blocker $\rightarrow$ Peripheral Oedema $\rightarrow$ Loop Diuretic $\rightarrow$ Hypokalaemia $\rightarrow$ Potassium Supplement.
* *Example 2:* Donepezil (Cholinesterase inhibitor) $\rightarrow$ Urinary Incontinence $\rightarrow$ Oxybutynin (Anticholinergic) $\rightarrow$ Cognitive Deterioration.
* *Example 3:* Diuretic/Vasodilator $\rightarrow$ Dizziness $\rightarrow$ Prochlorperazine $\rightarrow$ Parkinsonism / Fall.

## 3. Methodology & Evidence Grading System

The guideline adhered to the **NHMRC** guideline methodology, **AGREE II** standards, and the **GRADE** framework:

* **Evidence-Based Recommendations (EBR):** Requires high- or moderate-certainty evidence from clinical trials. **Zero recommendations were classified as EBR**, reflecting the worldwide lack of large, high-certainty deprescribing RCTs powered for hard clinical endpoints (mortality, hospitalisation).
* **Consensus-Based Recommendations (CBR):** Formulated through a formal, single-blinded **Delphi consensus process ($\ge 75\%$ agreement)** when trial evidence was low or very low certainty. Phrased as: *"We suggest..."*
* **Good Practice Statements (GPS):** Ungraded, actionable statements where indirect clinical evidence demonstrates that implementing the action clearly produces more benefit than harm.

## 4. Analysis of Fall-Risk-Increasing Drugs (FRIDs)

### 4.1 How Many FRID Classes Are There?

1. **Standard International Consensus (STOPPFall / EuGMS):**
   * Internationally, the recognized benchmark for FRIDs—developed by the European Geriatric Medicine Society (EuGMS) task force (*de Vries, Seppala, van der Velde et al.*, extensively cited in this document)—identifies **14 distinct drug classes**.
2. **Medication Classes Addressed in this Guideline:**
   * Within this guideline document, **14 specific classes/groups** of medications are directly evaluated and assigned deprescribing recommendations due to their contribution to falls, orthostatic hypotension, sedation, or postural instability.

## 5. Summary of Drug-Specific Deprescribing Protocols

### 5.1 Cardiovascular & Renal
* **Antihypertensives:**
  * *Hierarchy of withdrawal:* (1) Loop diuretics, centrally acting agents, peripheral alpha-blockers $\rightarrow$ (2) Aldosterone antagonists $\rightarrow$ (3) Thiazides, beta-blockers $\rightarrow$ (4) CCBs $\rightarrow$ (5) ACEi / ARBs.
  * *Tapering:* Beta-blockers and centrally acting agents (clonidine, moxonidine) must be tapered by 50% every 2 weeks to avoid rebound tachycardia and hypertensive crises.
  * *Restart thresholds:* SBP $\ge 150\text{ mmHg}$ or DBP $\ge 90\text{ mmHg}$ over 3 consecutive readings; restart immediately if SBP $\ge 180\text{ mmHg}$ or DBP $\ge 110\text{ mmHg}$.
* **Diuretics (Furosemide):**
  * $< 40\text{ mg/day}$: Cease without tapering.
  * $40\text{ mg/day}$: Halve dose for 1 week, then cease.
  * $80\text{ mg/day}$: Halve dose for 2 weeks, then cease.
  * $80\text{--}160\text{ mg/day}$: Reduce by $40\text{ mg}$ every 2 weeks until reaching $40\text{ mg}$.
* **Statins (Lipid-Modifying Agents):**
  * Discontinue without tapering for primary prevention in adults aged 65–79 with 5-year CVD risk $< 5\%$, or $5\text{--}10\%$ with a CAC score of 0.
  * Deprescribe in primary or secondary prevention if life expectancy $< 12$ months, severe frailty, or statin-induced myopathy.
* **Digoxin & Sotalol:**
  * Deprescribe digoxin if HFrEF is stabilized on 4-pillar GDMT and patient is in sinus rhythm; stop abruptly if serum level is subtherapeutic.
  * Deprescribe sotalol in permanent AF; taper by 25% every 1–2 weeks.
* **Organic Nitrates:**
  * Deprescribe long-acting nitrates if angina-free and no PRN sublingual nitrate use for $\ge 6$ months on background antianginal therapy.

### 5.2 Antithrombotics
* **Aspirin (Primary Prevention):** Cease without tapering in adults $\ge 70$ years without established atherosclerotic CVD (ASPREE data: major bleeding outweighs vascular benefit).
* **DAPT De-escalation:** Stop P2Y12 inhibitor beyond 6–12 months post-ACS (or 1–3 months in high bleeding risk). Post-stroke DAPT is limited to 21 days.
* **Triple Therapy:** Step down within 1–4 weeks post-PCI to OAC + single antiplatelet, then to OAC monotherapy after 6–12 months.
* **Warfarin in Advanced CKD:** Deprescribe in stage IV–V / dialysis due to severe calciphylaxis and bleeding risks.

### 5.3 Bone Health & Endocrine
* **Bisphosphonates:** Offer a **drug holiday** after 5–10 years in patients with T-score $\ge -2.5$ and no vertebral fractures. Cease abruptly without tapering.
* **Denosumab (High-Alert Protocol):** **Never cease abruptly or give a drug holiday.** Stopping denosumab causes rebound bone turnover and multiple spontaneous vertebral fractures. Transition sequentially to a bisphosphonate (alendronate) starting 6 months after the final injection for at least 12 months.
* **Teriparatide:** Deprescribe at $\le 24$ months (lifetime safety limit); always transition to a bisphosphonate to lock in BMD gains.
* **Oral Corticosteroids (Prednisone/Prednisolone):**
  * Deprescribe long-term use in COPD (not indicated) and in PMR/RA once remission is achieved.
  * *Tapering:* Reduce by 10–20% weekly; when approaching physiological replacement ($4\text{--}6\text{ mg}$ prednisone equivalent), reduce by $1\text{ mg}$ every 4–8 weeks to prevent adrenal crisis.
* **Levothyroxine:** Deprescribe in asymptomatic subclinical hypothyroidism or prescribing relics (post-amiodarone). Halve dose for 6 weeks; verify TSH and free T4 before full cessation. Age-adjusted TSH target: $1\text{--}5\text{ mU/L}$ (age 65–80) and $4\text{--}6\text{ mU/L}$ (age $>80$).

### 5.4 Psychotropics & Analgesics
* **Antipsychotics:** In dementia BPSD, deprescribe at 12 weeks. Taper by 25% every 1–4 weeks, or use hyperbolic micro-tapering (5–10% drops) to avoid withdrawal dyskinesia.
* **Benzodiazepines & Z-Drugs:** Taper by 25% every 1–4 weeks. Combine with patient educational brochures (e.g., EMPOWER model) to overcome psychological dependence.
* **Antidepressants:** Deprescribe after 6–12 months of remission from major depression. Taper by 25% every 1–4 weeks; differentiate antidepressant withdrawal syndrome from true depressive relapse.
* **Opioids:**
  * Duration $< 12$ months: Taper by 5–10% MED weekly.
  * Duration $\ge 12$ months: Taper by 5–10% MED monthly.
  * Taper long-acting opioids first. Educate on reduced opioid tolerance to avoid accidental overdose if restarting.
* **Anti-Dementia Drugs:** Deprescribe cholinesterase inhibitors if treatment exceeds 12 months without demonstrable benefit or if end-stage dementia develops. Deprescribe co-administered anticholinergics first.

### 5.5 Respiratory & Sensory
* **Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS) in COPD:** Deprescribe ICS in patients on triple therapy who had 0 hospitalisations and $< 2$ moderate exacerbations in the preceding 12 months; continue maintenance LABA/LAMA.
* **Ophthalmic Steroids:** Discontinue after 6–8 weeks post-surgery.
* **Glaucoma Drops:** Deprescribe in palliative or limited life expectancy settings where disease progression will not impact lifetime functional vision.

## 6. Key Clinical Pearls & Summary

1. **Rebound Phenomena vs. Disease Relapse:** Anticipate predictable physiological withdrawal syndromes (PPI rebound acid hypersecretion, steroid-induced adrenal insufficiency, benzodiazepine rebound insomnia, beta-blocker sympathetic rebound) and distinguish them from genuine disease relapse.
2. **Sequential Antiresorptive Management:** Denosumab must never be stopped without an immediate bisphosphonate bridge.
3. **Primary Prevention Deprescribing:** Aspirin and statins for primary prevention offer diminishing net benefit and increasing absolute harm as chronological age and frailty advance.
4. **Interprofessional Collaboration:** Successful deprescribing requires coordination between general practitioners, accredited pharmacists (e.g., HMR/RMMR reviews), bedside nurses, specialists, and the patient.
